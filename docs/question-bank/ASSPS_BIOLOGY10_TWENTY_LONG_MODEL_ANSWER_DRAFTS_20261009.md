## Proposed student-facing explanations (faculty must independently review)

### X-BIO-2026-C01-B01-L01 — Chapter 1, Nutrition and its Importance

Food first enters the digestive tract by ingestion. Mechanical actions and digestive enzymes break it into smaller, soluble molecules during digestion. Most digested nutrients are absorbed across the lining of the small intestine into blood or lymph. Circulation delivers them to cells, where they are assimilated into body materials or used for energy and other functions. Material that remains undigested is eventually expelled through egestion.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Ingestion introduces food · Physical and chemical digestion · Nutrient absorption across intestinal lining · Transport and assimilation into cells · Egestion of undigested remnants

### X-BIO-2026-C01-B02-L01 — Chapter 1, Human Digestive System

The alimentary canal transports food from the mouth through the oesophagus, stomach, intestines and rectum. Chewing and muscular mixing help mechanical digestion, while enzymes and other digestive secretions break large molecules into absorbable units. Peristalsis propels the contents along the tract. The small intestine has a large folded surface with villi that supports efficient nutrient absorption into blood and lymph. The large intestine absorbs much of the remaining water before waste leaves the body.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Ordered alimentary canal and propulsion · Mechanical digestion · Chemical enzyme-driven digestion · Small intestinal villi and absorption · Nutrient transport and large-intestine water uptake

### X-BIO-2026-C02-B01-L01 — Chapter 2, Human Respiratory System

During inhalation, the diaphragm contracts and flattens while the rib cage generally moves upward and outward. This increases chest volume, lowers air pressure in the lungs below atmospheric pressure and draws air inward. During quiet exhalation, these muscles relax, chest volume falls and air moves outward. At the alveoli, oxygen diffuses into blood because of a partial-pressure gradient, while carbon dioxide diffuses from blood into the air spaces. Blood transports oxygen mainly attached to haemoglobin and carries carbon dioxide back toward the lungs.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Diaphragm and rib action during inhalation · Chest volume and pressure cause inflow · Reverse changes during quiet exhalation · Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffusion at alveoli · Blood transport of respiratory gases

### X-BIO-2026-C02-B02-L01 — Chapter 2, The Mechanism of Breathing

In inhalation the diaphragm contracts and becomes flatter and the external intercostal muscles lift the ribs. The increased thoracic volume reduces pressure inside the lungs, so air flows inward. During normal passive exhalation the diaphragm and inspiratory muscles relax, the ribs return toward their resting position and the lungs recoil. Thoracic volume decreases and pressure rises relative to the atmosphere, driving air out. Thus the two phases differ in muscle activity, chest volume, pressure and direction of airflow.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Inhalation diaphragm contraction · Inhalation rib movement · Volume increase and pressure drop · Quiet exhalation relaxation and recoil · Volume/pressure reversal and outward flow

### X-BIO-2026-C03-B01-L01 — Chapter 3, Blood and its Components

Blood contains a liquid part called plasma and several types of formed elements. Plasma carries dissolved nutrients, hormones, salts, proteins and many waste products. Red blood cells contain haemoglobin, which transports most of the oxygen. White blood cells help protect the body by recognizing and responding to infection. Platelets, together with clotting proteins in plasma, help form blood clots that limit bleeding after damage to a vessel.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Plasma and dissolved substances · Red blood cells and haemoglobin · White blood cell defense · Platelets and haemostasis · Cooperation with plasma clotting proteins

### X-BIO-2026-C03-B02-L01 — Chapter 3, Human Heart

Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium through the venae cavae, then passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle. The right ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary valve and pulmonary arteries to the lungs. Oxygenated blood returns through pulmonary veins to the left atrium and passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle. The left ventricle drives blood through the aortic valve and aorta to the systemic circulation. Valves help maintain one-way blood flow through the heart.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Venae cavae and right atrium · Tricuspid valve/right ventricle to pulmonary arteries · Lungs and pulmonary venous return · Left atrium/mitral valve/left ventricle · Aorta to body and one-way valves

### X-BIO-2026-C04-B01-L01 — Chapter 4, Functioning of the Kidney

Blood reaching the glomerulus is filtered under pressure, allowing water and small solutes to enter the nephron while most blood cells and large proteins remain in circulation. As filtrate moves through the tubules, useful glucose, amino acids, salts and much water are selectively reabsorbed into the blood. Additional substances, including certain ions and wastes, may be secreted from blood into the tubules. The nephron adjusts water and ion recovery according to the body’s needs. The remaining fluid becomes urine containing wastes and excess water or salts.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Glomerular filtration · Selective return of useful solutes · Tubular water and ion regulation · Secretion into tubule · Final urine composition

### X-BIO-2026-C04-B02-L01 — Chapter 4, Human Urinary System

Urine begins as filtered fluid within nephrons of the kidneys. After reabsorption and secretion, the remaining urine flows through collecting ducts toward the renal pelvis. Each kidney sends urine down a ureter by muscular contractions. The urinary bladder stores the urine until an appropriate time for elimination. During urination, urine passes through the urethra to leave the body.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Nephrons form urine · Collecting ducts and renal pelvis · Ureters transport urine · Urinary bladder stores it · Urethra provides exit

### X-BIO-2026-C05-B01-L01 — Chapter 5, Neurons - The Units of Nervous System

A stimulus is detected by a receptor, such as a sensory ending in the skin. The receptor produces signals carried by a sensory neuron to the central nervous system. The spinal cord or brain processes the information, sometimes through interneurons, and selects a response. A motor neuron conducts a signal from the central nervous system to an effector. The effector, such as a muscle or gland, responds by contracting or secreting a substance.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Stimulus and receptor · Sensory neuron transmission · Central processing/interneuron · Motor neuron output · Muscle or gland effector response

### X-BIO-2026-C05-B02-L01 — Chapter 5, Endocrine System

Nervous coordination uses electrical impulses along neurons and chemical neurotransmitters at synapses, whereas endocrine coordination uses chemical hormones carried mainly in blood. Nerve signals follow defined neural pathways and usually act rapidly, often for a short time. Hormonal signals commonly develop more slowly and may last longer. Neural responses can target particular muscles or glands through their nerve connections; hormones reach many tissues but affect cells with the appropriate receptors. Both systems coordinate body functions and can work together.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Electrical/neurotransmitter versus hormonal signals · Neural pathway versus bloodstream route · Fast versus generally slower response · Brief versus often longer-lasting effects · Specific innervation versus receptor-defined hormonal targets

### X-BIO-2026-C06-B01-L01 — Chapter 6, Formation of Gametes and Fertilization

Ordinary human body cells usually contain two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent. During meiosis, germ cells produce gametes with one set of chromosomes rather than two. A sperm and egg each contribute a haploid set when they fuse during fertilization. Their union produces a diploid zygote and restores the chromosome number typical of the species. Meiosis also reshuffles genetic information through segregation and recombination, contributing to differences among offspring.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Diploid body-cell chromosome sets · Meiosis halves chromosome number · Haploid sperm and egg · Fertilization restores diploid zygote · Segregation/recombination adds variation

### X-BIO-2026-C06-B02-L01 — Chapter 6, Role of Hormones in Sexual Development

At puberty, signals from the hypothalamus and pituitary gland stimulate the reproductive organs through a hormonal feedback system. The testes produce testosterone, which supports sperm production and changes such as voice deepening and body-hair development. The ovaries produce oestrogens and progesterone, which help coordinate the menstrual cycle and changes such as breast development. Pituitary hormones including FSH and LH regulate gamete formation and reproductive hormone production. Feedback between these glands and target tissues helps adjust hormone levels throughout reproductive life.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Hypothalamus and pituitary coordination · Testosterone and testicular function · Oestrogens/progesterone and ovarian function · FSH/LH and gamete regulation · Hormone feedback loops

### X-BIO-2026-C07-B01-L01 — Chapter 7, Structure of Chromosome

Chromosomes are structures made from long DNA molecules associated with proteins. DNA stores hereditary information in the sequence of its nucleotide bases. A gene is a region of DNA whose information contributes to a functional product, such as a protein or RNA. Before cell division, DNA is copied so hereditary information can pass to new cells. During reproduction, chromosomes in sperm and egg carry genetic information from each parent to the offspring.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Chromosomes contain DNA · DNA base sequence stores information · Genes are functional DNA regions · DNA replication and cell inheritance · Gametes transmit parental genetic material

### X-BIO-2026-C07-B02-L01 — Chapter 7, Mendel’s Laws of Inheritance

Consider a simple trait controlled by two alleles, A and a, where A is dominant. If two heterozygous parents, Aa and Aa, reproduce, each can make gametes carrying A or a. Combining these gametes gives the offspring genotypes AA, Aa, Aa and aa. Their expected genotype ratio is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. Under complete dominance, AA and Aa show the dominant phenotype while aa shows the recessive phenotype, producing an expected 3:1 phenotype ratio in a sufficiently large sample.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Define allele symbols and dominance · Both parental genotypes Aa · Gametes A and a · Punnett outcomes and 1:2:1 genotype ratio · Complete-dominance 3:1 phenotype ratio with assumption

### X-BIO-2026-C08-B01-L01 — Chapter 8, Genetic Engineering

In a general biotechnology workflow, scientists first identify the gene associated with a useful trait or biological product. A suitable delivery system, such as a carrier DNA molecule, helps transfer that genetic information into a host organism. The host cells that contain the intended genetic information are identified and examined for the expected result. Under appropriate controlled conditions, some host cells express the gene and produce the relevant molecule or trait. The product or trait is then evaluated for quality, effectiveness and biosafety before any permitted application.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Identify relevant genetic information · Choose delivery or carrier system conceptually · Introduce information into a host · Identify/verify and evaluate expression · Product effectiveness and biosafety review

### X-BIO-2026-C08-B02-L01 — Chapter 8, Applications of Biotechnology

Biotechnology is used in medicine, for example to produce certain therapeutic proteins, and in agriculture, for example to develop plants with useful characteristics. Medical production can improve access to a needed medicine, while agricultural applications may help protect crops or improve yield under particular conditions. Another application is diagnostic testing based on biological molecules, which can support disease detection. Potential benefits must be weighed against risks, including unexpected ecological effects, product safety and fair access. Careful testing, oversight and clear labeling or monitoring where required help manage those risks.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Two named applications · Benefit of first application · Benefit of second application · A scientifically plausible risk · Testing, ethics and monitoring considerations

### X-BIO-2026-C09-B01-L01 — Chapter 9, Pathogens

Pathogens such as certain bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites can enter a host through the respiratory system, contaminated food or water, damaged skin or other routes. They may multiply within the host or produce substances that damage tissues and disrupt normal functions. Symptoms arise from tissue damage and the body’s immune response. Transmission can occur through droplets, contaminated surfaces, food, water or vectors, depending on the pathogen. Handwashing, safe food and water, appropriate ventilation, vaccination when available and other route-specific public-health measures help reduce spread.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Pathogen and entry route · Multiplication or pathogenic activity · Host damage and immune symptoms · Relevant transmission mechanism · Appropriate matched prevention measure

### X-BIO-2026-C09-B02-L01 — Chapter 9, Immunity

Innate immunity provides rapid, broadly acting defenses, including skin barriers, inflammatory responses and phagocytic cells. Adaptive immunity uses particular lymphocytes to recognize specific antigens and may involve antibodies or specialized cellular responses. After exposure, some adaptive immune cells remain as memory cells that respond more quickly on later encounters. Vaccination presents the immune system with a safe form or component of a pathogen, or instructions for making an antigen, so adaptive protection can develop. Vaccination greatly reduces the risk of certain infections or severe disease without requiring the person to experience the full disease.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Innate broad/rapid defense · Adaptive antigen specificity · Lymphocytes and antibody or cellular actions · Immunological memory · Vaccine-induced protective response without full disease

### X-BIO-2026-C10-B01-L01 — Chapter 10, Theory of Evolution by Natural Selection

Individuals in a population show variation, some of which is inherited. Environmental pressures can make particular inherited traits more helpful for survival or reproduction than alternatives. Individuals with advantageous heritable traits may leave more surviving offspring on average, passing their alleles to later generations. Across many generations, the frequencies of those inherited variants can increase, although conditions can change. Natural selection therefore changes population characteristics over time rather than deliberately changing an individual because it needs to adapt.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Heritable variation · Selection pressures · Differential reproductive success · Inheritance and allele-frequency change · Population change across generations, not purposeful individual change

### X-BIO-2026-C10-B02-L01 — Chapter 10, Evidences of Evolution

Fossils reveal organisms that lived at different times and can show changes in body form across geological history. Comparative anatomy can identify homologous structures that share underlying features despite serving different functions, suggesting shared ancestry. Similarities and differences in DNA and protein sequences provide another independent way to estimate evolutionary relationships. Biogeography and some developmental patterns also offer clues about how related organisms diversified. When these different lines of evidence support a consistent branching history, confidence in the proposed relationships increases.

**Separate marking-point proposal:** Fossil timeline evidence · Homologous anatomical structures · Molecular sequence comparisons · Biogeographic or developmental evidence · Independent lines supporting a shared ancestry hypothesis
